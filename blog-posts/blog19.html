Critical Thinking Is Becoming Optional

Lately I have been thinking a lot about critical thinking. Not in the fake LinkedIn way, where everyone says “think critically” and then proceeds to repeat the same five opinions everyone else is repeating. I mean the real thing: the ability to look at information, question it, understand the trade-offs, and make a judgement call that you can actually defend.

Because that is the part I think we are slowly losing. And ironically, we are losing it at the exact moment where we have access to more information, better tools, and more intelligence on demand than ever before.

AI Is Not the Problem. Outsourcing Your Judgement Is.

I am not anti-AI. That would be stupid. AI is useful. Claude Code, Cursor, ChatGPT, Copilot, agents — all of these tools can make people faster, help them prototype, debug, write, research, and build things that would have taken much longer before.

But there is a difference between using AI to accelerate your thinking and using AI to avoid thinking.

I have already seen this happening: people rely too much on AI to make choices for them, and then when you ask why a certain decision was made, they struggle to explain it. The answer is basically: “Claude Code did that.”

That is dangerous, not because the tool is bad, but because the person becomes vulnerable. If you cannot explain the system, justify the trade-off, defend the design, or understand the implication of what you accepted, then what exactly are you bringing to the table?

At that point, we are not AI-augmented humans anymore. We are just human-shaped approval buttons.

And if judgement, consideration, and opinionated decision-making disappear from our work, then we might as well build agents to replace us. Because if all we do is accept outputs, forward summaries, and approve generated code without understanding it, we are not meaningfully different from the automation layer.

This is why I still believe judgement is becoming the most important skill in tech. Code is cheap now. Content is cheap. Reports are cheap. Slides are cheap. Summaries are cheap. But knowing what should exist, why it should exist, what trade-offs matter, what risks are hidden, and when the tool is confidently wrong — that is not cheap. That is the job.

Social Media Trained Us for This

AI did not create this problem from scratch. Social media prepared us for it.

For years, we have been trained to outsource our opinions to feeds, influencers, group identities, and algorithmic consensus. You open an app, you see what everyone is angry about, what everyone is praising, what everyone is mocking, and slowly you learn the safest position to take. Not necessarily the correct one. The safest one. That is a very different thing.

Social media makes us conformist in a very subtle way. It does not usually force us to agree. It just surrounds us with signals: likes, reposts, comments, outrage, social punishment, status, belonging. Over time, you start adjusting your beliefs not based on whether they are true, but based on whether they fit.

The scary part is that the majority is often wrong. History is full of people who were told they were crazy, naive, dangerous, or simply wrong — and later turned out to be right. Galileo was condemned for defending heliocentrism. Ignaz Semmelweis was mocked for arguing that doctors should wash their hands before treating patients. Many early critics of smoking, lead, or environmental damage were dismissed before the evidence became impossible to ignore.

The point is not that you should become a contrarian for sport. Most contrarians are just conformists with a different costume. The point is that you need the courage and willingness to look deeper. Sometimes the consensus is right. Sometimes experts are right. Sometimes the group knows more than the individual. Being conformist is not automatically wrong.

But blindly conforming is.

The people who will be fine are not the ones who disagree with everything. They are the ones who can pause and ask:

  • Does this actually make sense?
  • What evidence do I have?
  • What am I missing?
  • Am I believing this because it is true, or because it is socially convenient?

That habit is underrated.

I Am Lucky, and That Matters

I also want to be fair here. It is easy to criticize people for doomscrolling, but the reality is that not everyone has the same access to good information, good environments, or good habits.

I have been lucky. I have access to relatively high-quality, less noisy information. For example, I can start my morning by reading Bloomberg Terminal top news instead of trying to understand the world through Instagram stories, TikTok clips, or random Twitter threads.

That makes a difference. It is much easier to think clearly when your inputs are cleaner.

I also do not really use social media much, and I think that helps. It probably makes me less biased by online consensus and less conformist than I would otherwise be. Not because I am somehow smarter or morally superior, but because I am simply exposed to less noise.

That is the uncomfortable truth: your information diet shapes your mind. If your inputs are mostly rage, comparison, hot takes, algorithmic outrage, and people performing certainty for status, your thinking will eventually reflect that. You cannot consume noise every day and expect clarity to magically appear.

But this is not about shame. If you use social media a lot, you should not feel ashamed. These platforms are designed to be addictive. They are built by extremely smart people whose job is to keep you there. The point is not to feel guilty. The point is to become aware.

Awareness is the key. Once you realise how much your thoughts are being shaped by your environment, you can start changing the environment.

The Real Problem Is Passive Living

The common thread across AI, social media, and algorithmic culture is passivity. You stop choosing.

You let the model write the answer. You let the feed decide what matters. You let the group decide what is acceptable. You let the algorithm decide what you want. You let the tool decide what you think.

And because all of this feels convenient, it does not look like a problem at first. It feels efficient.

But efficiency without awareness is dangerous. You can move very fast in the wrong direction. You can generate a lot of work without understanding it. You can hold strong opinions without knowing where they came from. You can sound informed while being completely captured by whatever your feed decided to show you this week.

That is why critical thinking matters so much now: not as an academic concept, but as self-defense.

Some Considerations

I do not think the answer is to reject AI. Use AI. Use it a lot. But stay in charge.

Ask it questions. Challenge it. Make it explain assumptions. Use it to test your thinking, not replace it. If it writes code, understand the code. If it drafts a message, understand the message. If it gives you a recommendation, understand the trade-off.

The same applies to information. Read longer things. Read people you disagree with. Read sources that are not optimized for engagement. Take breaks from feeds. Let yourself be bored. Boredom is underrated because it gives your mind space to form its own thoughts.

And most importantly, practice explaining your decisions. If you cannot explain why you chose something, you probably did not choose it properly. That applies to code, work, investing, opinions, relationships, politics, everything.

The future will not reward people who can simply produce more output. AI will produce infinite output. The future will reward people who can think clearly, judge well, and stay mentally independent in environments designed to make them reactive and conformist.

Critical thinking is not dead yet. But it is becoming optional. And that is exactly why it is becoming valuable.
